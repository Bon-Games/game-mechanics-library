Social stealth makes being seen different from being recognized as a threat. Joining a civilian group, adopting an accepted role, or performing an ordinary activity can prevent identification. Leaving that context or behaving suspiciously can expose the player; a disguise is not necessarily permission to enter every area. Unlike hiding behind a wall, the protection comes from appearing to belong.

## Where it is used

Especially suited to urban stealth-action adventures and assassination sandboxes, where crowds, social roles, and guarded public spaces make infiltration possible in plain sight. Crowd blending and role-based disguises are alternative implementations, not requirements every game must combine.

## Example

An assassin joins a merchant group moving through a guarded entrance. Staying with the group maintains cover; stepping away ends the blending protection and risks identification. The reference video demonstrates this merchant-group approach before an attack in Mirage.

## Why developers use it

- **Design:** Turn people and routines into navigable cover, creating choices about routes, timing, and when to break character instead of relying only on dark corners.
- **Development:** Keep blending, suspicion, and area permissions consistent, with readable feedback when protection starts or ends. Test crowd movement and detection together.
- **Production:** Budget for civilian behaviors, animations, permitted-role rules, and encounter QA. Reusable routines can support multiple approaches, but crowd density and exception cases add performance and testing costs.

## What the player sees and feels

Civilian groups, mundane activity prompts, disguise options, and blending or suspicion cues communicate where the player belongs. Moving near guards without attracting attention can feel like a convincing deception; losing cover creates tension. Inconsistent recognition rules can instead feel arbitrary. These experience goals and production tradeoffs are editorial synthesis, not guaranteed outcomes.
